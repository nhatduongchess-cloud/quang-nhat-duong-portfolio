CARLA ADAS — active safety and Level 3 automation

2026

An end-to-end driving stack in the CARLA simulator: multi-sensor perception, a geometry-first emergency braking corridor swept through LiDAR rather than gated on a detector, a committed safety arbiter, and a reinforcement-learned cruise controller that can only ever propose a speed under a hard safety cap.

Scenario suite
43 / 45
Full catalog × 3 seeds against a live server. Zero collisions and zero sensor-frame errors across all 90 runs.
Weather matrix
28 / 30
Core scenarios × 5 weather profiles. Both suites fail on the same marginal scenario, not on a range of them.
Offline test suite
204 + 189
Safety self-test checks plus unit tests, all run in CI without a simulator, alongside lint and static typing.

An earlier run of the same suite reported 45/45. That number was measured with the reaction-delay criterion effectively switched off — the harness had no hazard origin to time from, so it could not fail. Once it was fixed, two cases started failing. The lower number is the honest one and it is the one published, with the open finding written up rather than tuned away.

A decision I made: braking triggers on metric geometry, not on detector confidence, so a missed bounding box cannot by itself cause a missed brake. The learned controller sits underneath that envelope and can never widen it.

Guardian Co-Pilot — driver monitoring

2026

A driver-state layer that sits above the ADAS and reads the person as well as the road: eyelid closure over a rolling window, the longest unbroken closure, and jaw opening — then explains every intervention in one sentence. Runs entirely on the edge.

60 unseen drivers
0.53
Held-out macro-F1, recurrent model, subject-disjoint 5-fold. The hand-written rule scores 0.51 on the same split.
Overfit gap
+0.20
In-sample minus held-out. A gradient-boosted baseline scores 1.00 in sample and 0.51 held out — a gap of +0.49.
Cross-dataset control
0.99 → 0.49
An image classifier trained with a random frame split, then tested on a different dataset. It lands on chance.

Read the first column as the real result: on drivers the model has never seen, this problem is hard, and the honest score is near the low end. The control experiment in the third column is the reason the numbers are reported this way — the same pipeline evaluated carelessly would have produced 99%, and that 99% would have meant nothing.

A decision I made: validation folds are split by driver, not by frame. Splitting by frame puts the same person on both sides and turns a memorised face into an apparent result.

A separate rule-based engine, measured frame by frame on 6 labelled drives (3,600 frames), catches 99.2% of impairment frames with a 9.8% false-alarm rate and 1.05 s median lag. Six drives is a demo, not a generalisation claim — the timeline for every drive, including where it disagrees with the ground truth, is on the demo page.
